title: Inequality, AI, and the Value of Knowledge
date: 2026-08-17
subtitle: What happens to a society in which only those who already have money can afford the years that knowledge requires?
summary: From today's inequality to AI's impact on work, the choices young people are already making, and the long-term social consequence: a division reproduced not by wealth alone, but by who can afford the time that knowledge costs.

## 1. The starting point: an increasingly unequal society

The French economist [Thomas Piketty](http://piketty.pse.ens.fr/en/)
argued in [*Capital in the Twenty-First
Century*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_in_the_Twenty-First_Century)
that when the return on capital outpaces economic growth, wealth
concentrates in the hands of those who already hold it. On his account,
the relatively egalitarian decades of the twentieth century were the
exception, not the rule. The idea confirms something we already sense: we
are returning to a social structure much like that of earlier centuries,
where a very small share of the population holds most of the wealth.

The problem is not only economic inequality, but also the **lack of social
mobility**. It is becoming harder and harder for anyone to significantly
improve their economic position through study, work, or a profession.

## 2. AI may deepen that process even further

On top of this comes something Piketty perhaps does not develop far
enough: the transformation of the labor market brought about by artificial
intelligence.

This is already underway. Many professional and administrative tasks are
being automated or losing their value, and the process will reach further
over the coming years. That introduces a new uncertainty: even careers
that historically offered economic security may stop doing so.

Our generation, one way or another, will probably pass through this
transition with some measure of protection. We entered the labor market
before it changed. **The bigger problem is for those coming after us.**

## 3. Young people are already reacting to that uncertainty

Many young people are starting to ask why they should invest so many years
in a traditional career (engineering, law, medicine) if that investment no
longer guarantees stability, prestige, or good income.

Hence the appearance of alternatives that look more pragmatic or more
profitable: gastronomy, entrepreneurship, gyms, content creation, YouTube,
social media.

This is not a hypothetical. A recent [DW
report](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xOUElf7catg) documents record
numbers of graduates entering labor markets where entry-level positions
are vanishing: 12.7 million new graduates in China this summer, 10 million
in India, and in several industries the first rung of the ladder is being
automated away. The OECD's Andreas Schleicher notes in the same report
that vocational training is becoming a faster route into work, and that
some employers now prefer candidates who adapt quickly over those holding
qualifications but little practical experience.

And in a certain sense, that decision can be perfectly rational from an
economic point of view.

## 4. The risk is not that they choose other jobs, but that they abandon intellectual development

The advice is now coming from inside the field. Geoffrey Hinton, whose
work on neural networks earned him the label of godfather of AI and who
left Google in 2023 to speak freely about its risks, [was asked what young
people should
study](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/godfather-of-ai-i-tried-to-warn-them-but-weve/id1291423644?i=1000713048391)
and answered that a good bet would be to train as a plumber. His reasoning
is that it will be a long time before machines match human physical
manipulation, while mundane intellectual labor is already being absorbed.
Coming from him, the recommendation is hard to dismiss as pessimism.

The problem would not be that someone prefers to be a cook, a trainer, or
a content creator. Those activities can also demand enormous talent and
knowledge. The problem is that **society stops valuing deep intellectual
training because it no longer offers a clear economic reward**.

But the perception is running ahead of the reality. What AI is dismantling
right now is the first rung: the junior analyst, the paralegal, the
entry-level developer. That is what a young person can see. What they
cannot see from there is whether the ladder still pays further up, and the
evidence that it does is much harder to read than the evidence that the
bottom has fallen out.

So the choice gets made on the visible part. If studying philosophy,
mathematics, history, science, literature, or a complex profession stops
being perceived as a path toward a better life, part of the population
will rationally decide not to develop those capacities.

And here is what makes the decision different from an ordinary mistake. A
trade takes the whole day, and it never asks for anything more. Nothing
punishes you for not studying. Nothing sets aside the years that formation
requires. Whether or not knowledge would have paid, the choice not to
pursue it removes the time and the pressure that pursuing it would have
needed. The belief was not necessarily true when it was acted on. Acting
on it makes it true afterwards.

A paradox could then emerge: a society far more advanced technologically,
but with an ever-smaller proportion of people devoted to developing
complex knowledge.

## 5. The consequence could be a new class structure

This could produce a social division unlike the traditional one.

On one side, the intellectual class: a small elite that owns the capital,
the technology, and the AI systems, together with the specialized minority
that designs and directs them. What they have in common is not knowledge
as such, but the condition that makes knowledge possible. Deep
intellectual formation costs years, and years have to be paid for. Whoever
lives on capital owns their own hours. Whoever lives on wages does not.

On the other, the working class: the majority working in services,
entertainment, care, trades, or physical and in-person activities that
cannot yet be easily automated.

And this division would be harder to cross than the old one. Knowledge is
still what allows movement between classes. But it is bought with time,
and time is exactly what the crossing would require. The obstacle is not
that people become incapable. It is that they are occupied, and that
nothing in the shape of the day ever asks them to be otherwise. The
division stops being a position and becomes an inheritance.

That is: **not only a concentration of wealth, but also a concentration of
knowledge and intellectual capacity.**

## 6. The most unsettling part

What is depressing about this scenario is that it seems difficult to
change at the individual level.

We can try to get our children to study, to read, to develop curiosity and
critical thinking. What we cannot give them is the thing that formation
actually costs. Years in which nobody has to earn are bought with money,
and that is precisely what makes the division an inheritance rather than a
position.

Which is why the only real defense was never individual. Public
institutions are what once made intellectual formation something other
than an inheritance, and they did it in a way that is easy to misremember.
They did not simply make knowledge free. Free knowledge matters when the
obstacle is the price of a book or a lecture, and that obstacle is already
gone: more is available now, at no cost, than any previous generation
could have imagined. The obstacle is the years, and no library gives those
away. What made Piketty's exceptional century exceptional was paid time.
Grants, stipends, cheap or free universities, the assumption that a young
person could spend years not earning. That is the part that is quietly
disappearing.

And the institutions are being pushed in the same direction as everyone
else. Degrees get shorter and more vocational, students work through them,
and the pressure everywhere is to reach employability faster. Not all of
that is wrong. Universities have always trained people for work, and
research that reaches the world is not thereby debased. But every
compression removes some of the unhurried time that formation requires,
and it removes it from exactly the people who have no other source of it.
Whatever the answer turns out to be, it will have to do what scholarships
and stipends once did: give people years they do not have to justify
economically. Everything else is a detail.

And perhaps the underlying question is this:

**What happens to a society in which only those who already have money can
afford the years that knowledge requires?**
