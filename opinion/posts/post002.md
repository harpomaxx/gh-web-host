Universities have a problem.

For a long time, they offered two promises at once. The first was access to
knowledge. The second was access to better work. If someone wanted to
become an engineer, a lawyer, a physician, or enter any number of
professional careers, the university was the place where the necessary
knowledge could be found and where society certified that it had been
acquired.

The two promises reinforced each other. Knowledge was difficult to obtain,
and those who possessed it were scarce. A degree therefore gave access not
only to a profession, but also to a better economic position.

That arrangement was never universal. University required opportunity,
preparation, discipline, and a certain kind of academic ability. Not
everyone could enter, and not everyone who entered could finish. Fairly or
not, the degree became a signal: the person holding it was assumed to know
things that most people did not and to be capable of work that most people
could not do.

Artificial intelligence is beginning to weaken that signal.

A surprising amount of practical knowledge is now accessible through
models. Anyone can request an explanation of a legal concept, help with a
program, a statistical analysis, or a description of a medical process and
receive a useful answer in seconds.

This does not mean that receiving an answer is the same as understanding
it. Someone with deep training will normally ask better questions,
recognize mistakes, and understand what a model leaves out. Established
knowledge and the effort required to learn it remain necessary.

The problem is what conventional academic work now proves. If a model can
solve the standard exercises and produce an acceptable version of the
final assignment, completing those tasks no longer demonstrates the same
capabilities it once did. AI does not erase the value of education. It
weakens the evidentiary value of a degree built mainly around recalling
information and reproducing known answers.

In one limited sense, **the undergraduate degree may be following the path
of the high school diploma**.

The analogy is not that AI will cause everyone to attend university. It is
that what once counted as advanced preparation can gradually become an
expected foundation. In the United States, the proportion of 18-year-olds
receiving a high school diploma rose from about 9 percent in 1910 to more
than half in 1940 (Goldin & Katz, 1997). University education later
experienced its own version of this transition as employers began demanding
bachelor's degrees for jobs that had not previously required them (Fuller &
Raman, 2017).

AI adds a different kind of pressure. It makes established knowledge and
routine intellectual work easier to access and reproduce. The degree may
therefore remain useful—even necessary—without remaining distinctive. If
that happens, universities will need to demonstrate something beyond
mastery of established material: the capacity to formulate a problem,
evaluate evidence, and work where no answer has been specified in advance.

If established knowledge becomes foundational rather than distinctive, the
next question is where intellectual value moves. The distinction between
reproducing a procedure and directing intellectual work is not new. In de
Prony's production of logarithmic and trigonometric tables, mathematicians
designed the methods while human “computers”—famously,
though perhaps apocryphally, described as unemployed hairdressers—performed
the routine arithmetic (Grattan-Guinness, 1990; Roegel, 2010). Leibniz made
the same distinction when he wrote that “it is unworthy of excellent men
to lose hours like slaves in the labor of calculation” (Leibniz,
1685/1929, p. 181).

AI extends that old division beyond arithmetic. As more routine execution
can be delegated, value moves toward choosing the problem, designing the
method, evaluating the evidence, and taking responsibility for the result.
Those are the capabilities a university degree must increasingly prove.

Universities should not stop teaching established knowledge. Without a
foundation, students cannot judge a model's answer or investigate a new
question. But transmitting that knowledge can no longer be the distinctive
endpoint of higher education. It must be the preparation for work in which
the answer is not already available.

The degree may therefore have to move in two directions at once: its taught
component becomes more like high school—a necessary foundation—while its
culminating work becomes more like research.

The bachelor's thesis is an obvious place to begin.

A thesis should provide evidence of what the student can do, not merely of
what they can submit. That requires a supervised process in which the
student formulates the problem, explains the decisions, confronts evidence,
and defends the result. The use of AI is not itself the problem. The
problem is work in which the student's intellectual contribution can no
longer be identified.

This does not mean that every undergraduate must produce a scientific
revolution. A serious thesis might study an unexplored local problem,
produce new evidence, run an experiment, construct and evaluate a real
system, or combine ideas in a way for which there is no predetermined
answer. The result need not be new to humanity, but the inquiry must be
authentic and the judgment must belong to the student.

The goal is not to demand more pages or make degrees artificially harder.
It is to change what difficulty means. Reproducing established answers is
becoming cheap. Choosing a meaningful problem, confronting reality, and
determining whether an answer deserves to be trusted remain difficult.

In this limited sense, the bachelor's degree may have to move closer to the
PhD. Not in duration, specialization, or the expectation of a major
scientific contribution, but in spirit: students should spend at least some
time working where no answer has been specified in advance.

Universities do not have to compete with AI at producing explanations of
what humanity already knows. They must teach the foundations and then test
what comes after: deciding which questions matter, recognizing plausible
but mistaken answers, and accepting responsibility for a conclusion.

After AI, a degree should not merely certify that its holder can produce an
answer. It should show that they can act when there is no settled answer—and
when an answer generated by a model is not enough.
